**

- The geo-political situation calls for resilient infrastructure to secure the future wealth and well-being of our society. Especially digital infrastructure as the relevant infrastructure of our time has to become more sovereign. Science can take a lead on this.
    
- Science has been a trail-blazer for social, economic and political progress. On the way towards a globalised community united by the pursuit of a universal still always open concept of scientific truth it has built close ties with many partners in the socio-economic and political field. These days it has become obvious for some of those former partners to be following a different agenda. Instead of a collective endeavour for scientific progress that benefits the wealth of all, those forces have now more or less openly terminated the unwritten contract to head for the accumulation of power for their own group, threatening to exclude anyone unwilling to bend to it.  
    Digitalisation or Artificial Intelligence would not exist without science and without massive investment by state agencies around the whole world. Yet some of the greatest and most powerful companies, that owe their success to technological developments only made possible by the global collaboration of scientists following the compass of the scientific system itself, believe they can dictate the future use of the jointly acquired knowledge (according to their private fantasies), only because they have managed to market it effectively and turn it into products.  
    To put it more specifically: The autonomy of the scientific system today is challenged by the growing and unprecedented influence of digital companies that are mainly based in the United States of America and controlling much of the infrastructure a science that is heavily driven by data relies on.
    

#### Section 1: Intro / Problem description 

This challenge has, as already said, become obvious today and also top-tier politicians (as president of the European Commission, Ursula von der Leyen) are addressing it, when talking about resilience and digital sovereignty. There also is a track record of initiatives that have been set up to tackle the issue. The European Open Science Cloud (EOSC) and the German Nationale Forschungsdateninfrastruktur (NFDI) are two prominent examples. Both aim at improving the handling of research data in the scientific domain as a whole. Making data interoperable across borders is a key objective here. Those borders may be between scientific disciplines, between states or countries or even between science and industry or other societal bodies. This is why they have committed themselves to the FAIR principles. This acronym not only stands for the terms Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable, but also for the vision of a fair usage of research data for the benefit of society as a whole.  
Everything is fine then? If you think David is well equipped to take on Goliath with his sling, it may well seem this way. But if you want to apply the image more closely to the current situation, you have to say that, in this case, the sling is hanging from Goliath’s belt and David has to ask him for permission to use it.**

**

- Just to put it into perspective in terms of numbers: The total budget of the NFDI initiative over the whole 9-year period (2019-2028) is around 900m Euro and that of EOSC is around the same size for the period from 2021-2030. On the other side, the four US companies Amazon, Google, Meta and Microsoft are said to have spent over 200 billion dollars on digital infrastructure in 2024 alone. This means the funding of the mentioned infrastructure programs (NFDI and EOSC) account for roughly one thousandth of the investment spent by commercial firms in the US for digital infrastructure.
    

#### Section 2: 

Of course the picture we’ve just painted is a bit skewed: There is no explicit opposition between US tech companies and the scientific system in Europe as in a fight. And the investment of the tech firms into digital infrastructure are aimed at the market for digital services as a whole. But still there is an imbalance as well as a dependency that is worrying even if it will never come to an open opposition.

**

In 2024 the director of the European Centre for International Political Economy (ECIP) in Brussels for example wrote of an $ 1.36 trillion investment gap between US and EU ICT (Information and Communications Technology) firms but concluded, that there is a mutual dependency, since “for US companies decoupling from these investments would mean losing access to a significant customer base, when there has already been extensive construction and operation of data centres by US companies across EU Member States.” For this reason politicians in Europe talking of digital sovereignty should be taken with a pinch of salt. The interdependencies between the different stakeholders are so tight and complex that they cannot be completely disentangled. But to act sovereignly does not imply that you have to be completely independent. It only means that you can make decisions by your own standards, without having to fear the consequences so much that you let yourself be dissuaded. This is why the path to greater sovereignty lies through increased resilience. Democratic institutions can remain sovereign only as long as they cannot be blackmailed by external forces too easily. The EU has decided upon a bundle of rules to secure its digital economy from such attempts and to grant the amount of self-determination and security to its citizens, that it deems necessary to safeguard their rights and dignity. Among those are the Digital Service and Digital Markets Act, the AI Act, the Cyber Resilience Act, the Data Act, the Chips Act and the Crypto-assets rules. This immediately led to accusations from the US government that the EU was treating US tech firms unfairly. 

Meanwhile some of those are responding in a smarter way to EU regulations by offering products, they say, fulfill the criteria of digital sovereignty (privacy, data-protection, cyber-security, resilience, transparency)  much better than any European solution could do (like Amazon does with the AWS European Sovereign Cloud). These offerings are clearly an improvement over unregulated services that violate the rights of EU citizens using them, still they are designed to maintain the structural imbalance and with it a certain dependency, just like tobacco firms developing “healthier” alternatives to tobacco want to keep their customers addicted to their products.

*Function: Evidence. Show the working machine. Convert sceptics by pointing to concrete progress.*

- **The bet NFDI made:** federated, community-driven, discipline-specific consortia rather than a top-down central data lake.
  *Function:* Explains the architectural choice; pre-empts "why not just one big platform?".
- **What has actually been achieved (concrete, factual):**

  - A measurable cultural shift: RDM moving from afterthought to core scholarly practice.
  *Function:* Replaces vague "success" with verifiable outcomes.
- **The limit of the consortium model:** disciplinary depth ≠ cross-disciplinary interoperability. Without shared *basic* services, every consortium reinvents IAM, PIDs, terminology services, training infrastructure.
  *Function:* Names the problem Base4NFDI was created to solve.
- **Base4NFDI as the connective tissue:** integrates and establishes basic services *as common goods* — IAM4NFDI, TS4NFDI, PID4NFDI, etc. — through a structured, community-driven integration process. [2], [3]
